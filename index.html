Part D · Reflection: Are all conversational AI systems essentially the same?

No. They are alike in some ways, but they are not the same.

They are alike because all three gave good answers. In Part B, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude all got 72 kWh for the computer lab problem. All three also kept the cloud computing answer under 150 words and used three short bullets for the AI question. In Part A, all three could answer questions, read files and images, use the web, and remember the chat.

They are different in style and features. ChatGPT gave short, direct answers. Claude added more detail, like a note that real computers do not use full power all the time, and its email had many blanks to fill in. Gemini put its email in a Gmail draft. Claude also needs an account, but ChatGPT and Gemini did not. My scores were 25 for ChatGPT, 23 for Claude, and 20 for Gemini, but the gaps were small.

In Part C, “Tell me about cybersecurity” gave a long list. When I added the audience, word limit, and format, the answer became shorter and clearer, with one example for each threat.

So the AI matters, but the prompt matters too. I should test the answers and not choose only by what I like.

No incorrect information was found in the three references. 4. Why is hallucination particularly problematic in academic work?

Hallucination is problematic because AI can generate false information and present it as if it were factual. Using fabricated references can affect the credibility of research, mislead readers, and damage a student’s academic reputation. 5. What verification practices should be followed before using AI-generated references in an academic paper?

Students should search for each reference using Google Scholar, Crossref, PubMed, or official publisher websites. They should compare the article title, authors, publication year, journal name, and DOI to ensure that all details match. They should also open the original publication and read its contents to confirm that it genuinely supports their research topic.

Part G · Final reflection: Why should conversational AI outputs be evaluated rather than automatically trusted?

Throughout this laboratory exercise, one thing I have learned is that AI is a powerful tool, but its outputs should always be double-checked rather than trusted completely without fact-checking first. AI may seem fluent, organized, structured, and relevant in its responses, but this does not guarantee that the information is factual or appropriate. These observations demonstrate that users must verify AI-generated information and apply critical thinking before relying on it completely.

One important observation came from the experiment involving false information. I asked the AI to explain why Java was created by Microsoft in 1991. It recognized that the prompt contained incorrect information instead of accepting the false premise as true. The AI corrected the statement by explaining that Java was actually created by Sun Microsystems under the leadership of James Gosling, while also clarifying the historical relationship between Java and Microsoft. This experiment showed that AI can identify incorrect assumptions, but users should still verify factual claims using reliable sources.

Another observation involved academic source recommendations. The AI generated three references that appeared credible by providing the authors, titles, publication years, journals, and DOIs. However, these sources still required human verification because AI can sometimes generate fabricated or inaccurate citations despite presenting them confidently. This demonstrated that information that appears professional and convincing may still contain errors, especially in academic and research-related tasks where accuracy is essential.

Beyond these experiments, the laboratory as a whole reinforced the importance of verifying AI-generated information before accepting it as true. Although AI can provide useful explanations, recommendations, and well-structured responses, it should be viewed as a tool that assists learning rather than a source of unquestionable truth. Human evaluation and critical thinking remain essential for checking facts, identifying possible errors, and making informed decisions based on reliable evidence.

Final reflection

What I learned about prompting

Draft written from the evidence above. To be updated after Module 4.

This portfolio began as a stack of lab activities and became a record of how my prompts changed. The clearest lesson came from the cybersecurity experiment in Lab Exercise #1. The same platform gave a broad overview to “Tell me about cybersecurity” and a short, example-driven answer once I named the audience, the length, and the format. The AI did not get smarter between those two prompts; my instructions did. Lab Exercise #2 showed the other side: “Which one is better?” got only a question back, because I had given it nothing to work with. The zero-, one-, and few-shot lab added a detail: examples did not change whether the answers were correct, but they controlled the style of the output, and a badly chosen example can give the answer away.

The Prompt Vault pushed that idea further. Writing prompts that could be tested, such as exactly three steps, twenty words per step, and a final line that must begin with ESCALATE:, forced me to turn wishes into checkable rules. The Broken Bot Labyrinth showed why those rules matter outside a classroom. The final boss was a bot ready to change a grade it had no authority to change, and the right fix was a boundary, a plain explanation, and a path to the professor and registrar.

I also learned to check the AI’s own work. The game master reported the same wallet balance at every level, and its estimate of about 20 tokens for Lock 1 did not fit an 88-word prompt. In Lab Exercise #2, the AI corrected a false claim about Java right away, and the three academic references it recommended turned out to be real, but I only knew that because I checked the main claims and every reference against sources. Smaller catches count too: our team’s registration left out a member, and we had the ledger regenerated. In Assignment #1 and Lab Exercise #1, I also noticed that my own conclusions leaned on style (“straight forward”) more than on scored criteria, so I now tie each judgment to a criterion and an example.

The extra lab applied the same ideas to an agent that acts on files. Test 1 was the clearest lesson: my safety rule said to ask when unsure, my prompt said not to ask, and the Skill did not say which won, so the agent guessed. The fix was one sentence that put the rule above the instruction, and the retest showed it worked. Checking the agent’s study notes against the slides also turned up two small errors, a count (twelve parts including the LLM, not around it) and a quote that came from speaker notes instead of the visible slide.
